1. 🌴 Cuba & the Caribbean 7

The clave family and its relatives. These are timelines: unvarying reference patterns that the rest of an ensemble orients to, rather than parts that develop.

#tresillo Pan-Caribbean

Three strokes over eight pulses, grouped 3+3+2. The most widespread rhythmic cell in Afro-Atlantic music: it underlies the Cuban son, Puerto Rican bomba, Brazilian maxixe, the New Orleans second line and modern reggaeton. In Cuban practice it is the first half of the son clave.

#claveSon Cuba

The five-stroke key pattern of Cuban son, and through it of salsa and most Afro-Cuban popular music. Clave means key or keystone: every other part in the ensemble orients to it. Its third stroke falls on step 7 — move that one stroke to step 8 and you have the rumba clave.

#rumba Cuba

The rumba clave, spine of secular Afro-Cuban drumming — guaguancó, yambú and columbia. It differs from the son clave by a single stroke, the third landing on step 8 rather than 7, which gives it a harder push against the pulse. It took shape in the solares of Havana and Matanzas in the nineteenth century.

#tumbao Cuba

The habanera cell, and the bass tumbao derived from it. It travelled out of Cuba into Mexican danzón and Argentine tango, and — as Jelly Roll Morton’s “Spanish tinge” — into early jazz. Coypu’s name for it reflects its later life as a bass pattern in son and salsa.

#banda Haiti · Cuba · Guinea

Five strokes in eight: cinquillo in Cuba, banda in Haitian Vodou drumming, and part of moribayasa among the Malinke of Guinea — three names for one rhythm, and a neat illustration of Atlantic circulation. Structurally it is tresillo with the gaps filled in.

2. 🇧🇷 South America 2

Two key patterns from the Atlantic coast of South America, both descended from the same Afro-Atlantic timeline tradition as the Cuban claves.

#bossa Brazil

The bossa nova clave, from the Rio de Janeiro of the late 1950s — in essence João Gilberto’s guitar reduction of samba. Its five strokes sit as evenly as sixteen steps allow: the pattern usually cited as the Euclidean rhythm E(5,16). Note that Coypu’s own #(5 16) euclidean uses a Bresenham variant yielding a rotation of this rather than the bossa pattern itself — use bossa when you want the clave.

Bell and drum timelines from the traditions the Caribbean patterns descend from — plus one twentieth-century popular form, soukous, that travelled back across the Atlantic in the other direction.

#soukous Congo

The pattern behind Congolese soukous — the guitar-led dance music that grew out of Cuban records re-imported to Kinshasa and Brazzaville in the 1940s and 50s, then swept the continent. The paired strokes give it a forward lurch.

#adowa Ashanti · Ghana

Ashanti funeral and ceremonial music from Ghana, played on atumpan talking drums with a bell. Coypu’s pattern is back-weighted — its strokes fall at the end of the cycle, pulling toward the downbeat that follows.

#bembe Yoruba · Afro-Cuban

In Afro-Cuban practice bembé names a Yoruba-derived ceremonial music whose bell pattern is normally a seven-stroke timeline in twelve pulses. Coypu implements Godfried Toussaint’s sixteen-pulse reinterpretation, which keeps the syncopation while fitting a binary grid. It is an adaptation, not the ternary original — and it is exactly what #(6 16) euclidean produces, so the Euclidean claim is verifiable rather than decorative.

4. 🪗 Balkans & Anatolia 2

Additive metres, which group pulses unevenly instead of subdividing a bar evenly. Neither fits a sixteen-step grid, which is the point of them.

#aksak Balkans · Anatolia

Aksak is Turkish for “limping” — the family of Balkan and Anatolian metres that group pulses in uneven twos and threes rather than evenly. Coypu’s source calls this one a pseudo aksak: a ten-pulse approximation of the feel.

#trueAksak Balkans · Anatolia

A genuine additive metre in thirteen pulses rather than an approximation — and unplayable on any grid that assumes powers of two. This is the pattern to reach for when you want the real asymmetric lilt.

7. How to read these patterns

Each card shows the rhythm as a row of steps: a filled cell is a trigger, an empty one a rest, with a small gap every four steps to mark the beat. Underneath, three facts computed from the pattern itself.

The ▶ button beside each row plays the pattern as a looping bell, at 120 BPM on a sixteenth-note grid, so you can hear the shape before you type it. Playing a second rhythm swaps the pattern without stopping the audio, which makes it easy to compare two claves back to back. It plays the same source-derived steps the row draws — the sound and the picture cannot disagree. The two generative rhythms have no button, because they have no fixed pattern to play.

FactMeaning
stepsThe length of the underlying pattern. Multiply by this number, not by 16 — 13 trueAksak and 26 trueAksak are right, 16 trueAksak truncates mid-cycle.
onsetsWhich steps carry a trigger, counting from 1.
groupingThe gaps between successive onsets, wrapping round the cycle. This is the number that tells you what a rhythm actually feels like — tresillo’s 3+3+2 is the whole of its character.

A dashed tag under a card means the rhythm also accepts a note string, so you can give it pitches as well as a rhythm: 

16 claveSon: '36 38 42 46' to: #perc.

Not every rhythm has that variant. Those that do are tagged; the rest take the plain form and you add notes separately with notes:.

9. Troubleshooting

16 bomba: '36 38' raises doesNotUnderstand

A bug in the Coypu source: Integer>>bomba: never assigns its pattern variable, so it is nil when the method reads pattern size. The plain 16 bomba works fine — add notes separately with notes: until it is fixed.

A rhythm sounds truncated or lurches at the loop point

The receiver is not a multiple of the pattern length. trueAksak is 13 steps and aksak is 10, so neither divides 16. Use 13 trueAksak, 26 trueAksak, 10 aksak and so on.

adowa and sikyi sound identical

They are. Both carry the same eight-step pattern in the current source — see their cards above.

A jungle pattern does not line up with my other parts

All three are 32 steps, not 16. Give them a multiple of 32, and give anything running alongside them a length that divides into it.

The ▶ button does nothing, or greys itself out

The preview needs two hosts: the WebChucK module from cdn.jsdelivr.net and, separately, webchuck.wasm — about 5 MB — from chuck.stanford.edu. If either is blocked or slow the buttons disable themselves and the console carries a [coypu] warning. Nothing else on the page depends on it. The first press is also the slowest: it downloads the WASM and starts the audio engine.

I hear nothing on the first press, then it works

Browsers refuse to start audio until you have interacted with the page, so the engine is only built on that first press. If the page was loaded from file:// it will never work — the audio worklet and the WASM both need http:// or https://.

I cannot find a rhythm I am sure exists

Clear the filter — it hides whole regions. Beyond these 31, rhythms can also be generated rather than recalled: see Euclidean patterns and hex beats in Rhythms: Advanced.

How do I list the rhythms from inside the image?

Rhythm list returns a Dictionary of every selector carrying the <rhythmCreation> pragma, together with its comment. That pragma is also what this page is generated from.
